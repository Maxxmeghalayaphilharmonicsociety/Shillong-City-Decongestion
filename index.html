🌲 The Battle for Lum Shyllong: Inside the Crisis Over Shillong’s Fragile Water Catchment

Rising above the congested streets of Meghalaya’s capital, the north-facing slopes of Shillong Peak, known locally as Ka Dymmiew (the forest where sunlight rarely reaches the ground), have long functioned as the city’s silent lifeline. Beneath its dense canopy lie nine perennial and seasonal streams, including Um Jasai, Um Sohkhlur, and Wah Risa, which feed the drinking water supply for Greater Laban, Shillong city, and the Umiam reservoir downstream.

Today, that forest is at the center of an escalating environmental, legal, and political standoff. What the Meghalaya government promotes as a ₹151-crore flagship tourism project, the Shillong Peak Ropeway, has been met with sustained street protests, administrative clashes, and accusations that vital environmental safeguards were quietly sidelined to make way for commercial development.

📜 A Protected Forest Left in Legal Limbo

The roots of the current crisis stretch back twenty-five years. In 2001, the state government officially recognized Lawsohtun as one of Shillong’s last remaining urban lungs, recommending a complete ban on all construction, whether public, private, or military. Yet, for a quarter-century, the area was never formally notified under the Meghalaya Protection of Catchment Areas Act, 1990.

⚖️ Legal Standoff and Commercial Expansion

Following a 2021 National Green Tribunal (NGT) order halting a proposed Indian Army housing project, the state was directed to evaluate protecting the zone within four months, a process that remains unresolved. While officials cite the need for landowner consent under the 1990 Act, critics note that similar protections were swiftly applied elsewhere while Lum Shyllong remained exposed.

The ₹151-crore ropeway project, backed by the New Development Bank and PM-DevINE, bypassed mandatory EIA public hearings due to a 2022 central exemption for aerial ropeways. Although officials frame the 913-metre gondola as low-impact, project blueprints reveal plans for extensive multi-storey terminals, guest rooms, parking for over 300 vehicles, and commercial spaces requiring the felling of 200-plus trees. Local groups, including the Joint Action Committee (JAC), report that a seasonal water source behind the site has already dried up since tree clearing commenced.

🏛️ Political Fractures and a City at a Crossroads

The project has triggered institutional friction. The Khasi Hills Autonomous District Council (KHADC) sealed a construction site over missing trading licences, drawing a police FIR from the state Tourism Department in response. Meanwhile, shifting political alliances have pitted former conservation petitioners against active community protesters.

Despite mounting public pushback and acute seasonal water scarcity in Shillong, the government maintains that all clearances are valid, leaving the future of the capital's primary watershed hanging in the balance.

🎯 Conclusion: The Unfulfilled Court Order

The real issue with the government isn’t the new "Expert Committees". It’s a 5 year old court order left unfulfilled deliberately.

The recent assurances from State leaders that government departments have already conducted "detailed surveys and research" for the Shillong Peak Ropeway Project hides both the scientific reality of watershed ecology and the central legal issue at stake.

Conducting a genuine environmental baseline study for a fragile mountain watershed like Lum Shyllong which feeds nine primary drinking water sources for Shillong city and the Umiam reservoir cannot be reduced to surface-level engineering checks, tower-distance measurements, or rapid donor checklists. True ecological science requires multi-year hydrological monitoring, sub-surface aquifer mapping, and independent, peer-reviewed research on how felling over 540 trees and constructing multi-storey commercial infrastructure will permanently alter groundwater recharge.

More importantly, the government’s sudden push to form post-construction "expert committees" is a distraction from a far more fundamental question.

Why has the State Government suppressed and left unfulfilled the July 13, 2021 judgment of the National Green Tribunal (NGT)?

In OA 47/2017 and OA 93/2017, the NGT Eastern Zone Bench explicitly recognized the north-facing slopes of Shillong Peak as a critical carbon sink, a rare botanical gene pool, and the primary drinking water lifeline for Shillong.

The Tribunal directed the Forest and Environment Department to complete landowner consultations and issue a final decision within four months on notifying the area as a protected catchment zone under the Meghalaya Protection of Catchment Areas Act, 1990.

More than five years later, that statutory notification remains buried in administrative limbo.

By refusing to finalize the catchment notification even after enforcing the very same 1990 Act in Ganol and Umiam earlier this year, the government has kept Lum Shyllong in a deliberate legal vacuum.

Every clearance the State now cites, whether EIA exemptions or forest diversion approvals, relies on treating this vital watershed as ordinary land rather than a legally protected Critical Catchment Area.

The public does not need post-facto committees to study a water source after trees have already been cut and streams have begun drying up.

The government must first obey the law, place all hydrological methodologies in the public domain, and fulfill the 2021 NGT directive to notify and protect Shillong’s last urban water tower before irreversible damage is done.
